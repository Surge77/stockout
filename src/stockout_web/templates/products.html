Pick a shop and a day. Every item it stocks is scored at once and listed busiest first, each one judged against its own usual — so a big seller having a quiet day and a slow item having a busy one both show up.

How to read this list

The order is by predicted {{ unit }}, which is what fills a shelf. The band beside each row is something else: whether that number is high or low for that item in this shop. The two disagree often, and usefully — the busiest item on the list can still be having a quiet day.

Every item is a separate series with its own cut points and its own trend line, so “busy” never means “sells more than the other items”.

Nothing describing an item is published by the source — no name, no category, no price. An item is its number and its history, which is why this page identifies them by what they shift rather than by what they are.

Data runs to {{ observed_through }}. Later dates are answered as forecasts — each item's own trend line carries the level out there.

Why the order and the band disagree. The list is sorted by how many {{ unit }} each item will shift, which is what an order is made of. The band says whether that figure is unusual for that item — its days sorted into its own quietest, middle and busiest thirds. A big seller can top the list and still be having a quiet day for itself, and that is the row worth looking at twice.
The last column is measured against the item's own trend line at this date, not against its all-time average — the same baseline the bands are cut on, so the two are comparable. An item that has grown all year is not “busy” merely for being bigger than it was in 2013.

The count comes from {{ regressor|replace('_', ' ') }} and the band from {{ classifier|replace('_', ' ') }}. They are fitted separately and can disagree — a row can sit between its own cut points while the classifier calls it quiet. Neither corrects the other. {% if classifier_accuracy and class_floor %} On this dataset the band is the weaker of the two: it is right {{ '%.0f'|format(classifier_accuracy * 100) }} times in 100 against {{ '%.0f'|format(class_floor * 100) }} for always guessing the commonest band, so where the two split, the predicted count is the more defensible number. {% endif %}
